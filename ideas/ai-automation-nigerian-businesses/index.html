AI automation for Nigerian businesses: what to automate first

A practical guide to finding a useful first AI workflow, connecting it to your operations and measuring whether it actually helps.

The useful answer

Start with frequent information work that has a clear outcome and an easy review step: document intake, enquiry routing or searching approved internal knowledge. Map the workflow, establish a baseline, and run an assisted pilot before allowing the system to act independently.

What to take away

  • Choose the bottleneck before choosing the model.
  • Keep a person responsible for consequential actions.
  • Measure completion, correction and total effort, including review.

The useful question is where work gets stuck

AI projects become vague when the starting point is a technology rather than a task. An operations manager may say the team needs an assistant, while the real constraint is that every enquiry arrives without the information needed to resolve it. The most useful first conversation follows the work from arrival to completion.

Look for repeated reading, classification, searching, drafting or re-entry. Ask the people doing the task where they wait, what they copy and which exceptions consume their attention. You want a specific sentence: “When this document arrives, this person extracts these fields, checks them against this system and sends this response.” That is a starting point an engineering team can investigate.

Choose a workflow with a bounded job

A good first pilot has a recognisable input, a useful output and a way to catch a mistake. It should also happen often enough for the team to learn from real examples. A task with unpredictable judgement, unclear ownership or unreliable source records may need process work before automation.

The examples below are possible starting points, not claims about projects Neustar has completed. The right choice depends on your actual documents, tools and operating environment.

How we design AI and automation

Separate rules from judgement

Not every automation needs a language model. A fixed calculation, a known approval rule or moving a record between compatible systems may be better handled with conventional software. AI is useful when the input varies in language or structure and the system needs to interpret it. A good workflow may combine both approaches.

For example, a model might suggest the category of an incoming request. Ordinary application logic can then enforce the permission, record the action and route it to the right queue. This keeps a probabilistic interpretation separate from the rules that govern what the system is allowed to do. It also makes the workflow easier to inspect when something goes wrong.

Design around the way your business actually operates

For a Nigerian business, the practical brief may include intermittent connectivity, staff working across devices, mixed document quality and several disconnected tools. These conditions belong in the architecture discussion. A technically impressive demonstration can be fragile if it assumes every file is clean and every connected service is always available.

Decide what happens when the connection fails, a document cannot be read or a connected application rejects an update. A useful design has a visible exception queue, safe retries and a route back to a person. Also decide which records are authoritative, who may access them and whether sending them to an external model is appropriate. Those decisions should precede a production integration.

Give human review a real place in the system

“Human in the loop” is meaningful only when someone has enough context, time and authority to intervene. Show the original source beside an extracted value. Make the proposed action visible before approval. Record who approved it and give the person a simple way to correct the result. A vague warning at the bottom of a screen is not a review process.

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework offers a useful vocabulary for ongoing governance, context, measurement and management. It is a voluntary framework rather than a substitute for designing the details of your workflow. In practice, assign a named owner, define unacceptable outcomes and agree how the team will stop or change the system if the evidence calls for it.

Measure the whole task, including the work created

Time saved at one step can be lost in checking, correcting or resolving downstream mistakes. Before the pilot, record the current completion time, manual effort and common errors. During the pilot, track completed tasks, corrections, exceptions and the amount of review required. Compare similar work rather than a polished demonstration with an unusually difficult manual case.

Cost also extends beyond model usage. Integration, data preparation, staff adoption, monitoring and maintenance can influence whether the workflow is worth operating. Ask for an implementation proposal that names these parts. A useful pilot ends with evidence for the next decision: expand it, change it, or stop because a simpler approach serves the business better.

Write a small brief that can become a real pilot

Bring one workflow to the table rather than a company-wide list of AI ambitions. Include representative inputs, the tools involved and someone who understands the exceptions. Explain the outcome you want and what would make an automated result unacceptable. This gives the team a concrete basis for scope.

  • What triggers the task, and how often does it occur?
  • What information does it need, and who controls that information?
  • Which output is useful, and who can approve it?
  • What happens if the proposed result is wrong?
  • Which baseline and pilot measures will decide the next step?
