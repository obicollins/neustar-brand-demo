Custom software development in Nigeria: write a better brief before the first line of code

The early decisions that make a web platform, mobile product or business system easier to scope, build and operate.

The useful answer

A useful software brief explains the people, workflow, outcome, data and integrations before listing features. It defines a focused first release, clear acceptance conditions and who will operate the product after launch. That gives an engineering partner a sound basis for architecture, scope and cost.

What to take away

  • Describe the work before listing the screens.
  • Make integrations and data ownership explicit.
  • Plan operation, handover and change as part of delivery.

Begin with an outcome someone can observe

“We need an app” is an ambition. “Our customers should be able to track a request without calling three different people” is a problem a team can design around. The second statement makes the users, the information and the purpose of the software more visible. It also creates a way to judge whether the first release has helped.

Choose a few observable outcomes: less re-entry, a clearer approval trail, a task completed in one place or information that reaches the right person. You do not need to promise a numerical improvement before you understand the baseline. You do need agreement about what the product should make possible and who will recognise success.

Map the workflow before mapping the screens

Follow a real task from its trigger to its conclusion. Include the hand-offs, waiting, checks and workarounds. Ask what happens when information is missing or the normal path cannot be followed. These exceptions often reveal more about the required product than an attractive collection of screens.

A workflow also exposes permissions. The person creating a record may not be the person allowed to approve it. A customer should not necessarily see an internal note. Write these boundaries into the brief early. They affect the data model, interface, audit history and effort required to build a dependable system.

Treat integrations as part of the product

Custom software usually lives among other systems. Payments, identity, messaging, accounting and legacy records may shape a large part of the project. An integration is more than an API being available: someone must own the access, explain the data and agree what happens when the connected service is unavailable.

For each connection, identify which system owns the record, what information moves, in which direction and under which permission. Consider duplicate updates, delayed responses and failed transactions. A product that handles the ordinary path beautifully but leaves people stranded when a dependency fails needs a better design.

  • List every external service or existing application involved.
  • Identify the authoritative source for each important record.
  • Confirm that suitable API access and documentation exist.
  • Describe how a failed connection should appear to the user.

Choose an architecture the team can operate

An architecture should fit the expected workload, the people supporting it and the rate of change. A new project does not automatically need the most distributed design available. More independently deployed services also create more interfaces, monitoring and operational coordination. A coherent modular application can be a sensible starting point when the context supports it.

The decision should be explainable in ordinary language. Which part is expected to change? What needs isolation? What can fail together? Who will support the environment? Ask your engineering partner to record the reasons for the important choices, including what would cause the team to revisit them. That record can remain useful long after the first implementation.

Make the first release a complete, focused journey

A first release can be small and still be coherent. It should let a particular person complete an important task from beginning to end. A large collection of disconnected partial features can make adoption harder, even when the project has technically delivered many items from a list.

Define acceptance conditions around that journey. Include permission checks, error states and the behaviour of required integrations. Invite the people who will use the product to review it early enough for their feedback to influence the design. A release should provide evidence about assumptions, not simply mark the end of implementation.

Plan what happens after launch

Ask who controls the source repository, domain, hosting and third-party service accounts. Agree how deployment works, how an issue is reported and what documentation the operating team needs. These are normal parts of a software engagement, and they are easier to resolve while the brief is being written.

Ongoing cost includes infrastructure, external services, maintenance and the effort needed to support users. Avoid comparing proposals only by the initial build price. Compare what is included in discovery, design, integration, quality checks, documentation and handover. A clear scope gives both the business and the engineering team a more reliable basis for a working relationship.

Connect the product to its cloud foundation

A practical brief you can bring to an engineering partner

The strongest brief does not need to prescribe every technical solution. It should make the context legible enough for the right questions to emerge. Include examples of current records and workflows, identify the decision-makers and distinguish essential requirements from preferences.

Neustar brings product design and systems architecture into the same discussion. We can help turn a loosely defined idea into a buildable plan, then carry that thinking through to the product itself.

  • The business problem and the outcome you want.
  • The people, roles and end-to-end workflow.
  • The information, integrations and access requirements.
  • The first complete journey and its acceptance conditions.
  • The constraints, decision owners and handover expectations.
